[P000] TAMNet: Tumor-Aware Multi-Task Representation Learning for Brain MRI Classification and Segmentation with Uncertainty Quantification
[P001] ABSTRACT— Accurate brain tumor analysis from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is important for automated diagnosis. This paper presents TAMNet, a unified framework that couples a shared convolutional encoder with an auxiliary segmentation head and Monte Carlo dropout for four-class brain MRI classification — glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor and no-tumor — together with predictive uncertainty. Our earlier measurements of this system were obtained from a train/test split that shared near-duplicate slices, so every headline number in that protocol was inflated. We therefore report a complete re-evaluation under a leakage-audited protocol: 41 training runs, one to five seeds per configuration, a held-out test set of 1672 images, and paired bootstrap confidence intervals on every comparison. Under this protocol TAMNet reaches a test accuracy of 0.9360 ± 0.0110 and a macro-F1 of 0.9355 ± 0.0109. This is statistically indistinguishable from a plain DenseNet-121 baseline (0.9360 ± 0.0125; paired Δ = +0.18 pp, 95 % CI [-0.90, +1.32] pp, p = 0.770) and significantly below EfficientNetV2-S, ConvNeXt-Tiny, Swin-T and ResNet-50 (Δ from -1.67 pp to -2.33 pp, all p = 0.006). Removing near-duplicates from the two public evaluation sets lowers accuracy to 0.9431 ± 0.0073 (Kaggle, 422 images) and 0.9234 ± 0.0218 (BRISC, 431 images). The ablations do not support the design as intended: the auxiliary segmentation loss gives no classification gain, channel attention is disabled in the executed model, and the learned fusion weights collapse onto the global pathway (α_global = 0.9707). We conclude that TAMNet does not outperform modern classification backbones on this benchmark, and we release the full re-evaluation, the de-duplication manifests and the corrected calibration and robustness measurements so that this negative result can be checked.
[P002] Index Terms— brain tumor, magnetic resonance imaging, multi-task learning, attention mechanisms, uncertainty quantification, data leakage, benchmark validity.
[P003] I.  INTRODUCTION
[P004] Brain tumor classification and segmentation from MRI support diagnosis, treatment planning and follow-up. Modern convolutional and transformer backbones reach high reported accuracy on public brain-MRI benchmarks [1]–[9], and the field has largely converged on a recipe: a strong image classifier, an optional segmentation branch, and — more recently — a mechanism for saying how confident the model is.
[P005] Two limitations persist. First, classification and segmentation are usually optimized as separate pipelines, so the segmentation information never shapes the representation used for diagnosis. Multi-task designs that share an encoder and add an auxiliary segmentation head have been proposed to make that representation tumor-aware [17]–[21]. Second, a predicted label is normally reported without any measure of reliability, even though calibration degrades under dataset shift [25]–[32]. Monte Carlo dropout [25] offers a cheap way to attach predictive uncertainty to an existing network without changing its training regime.
[P006] We built TAMNet on exactly that recipe. A DenseNet-121 encoder supplies a feature map; a spatial attention module and an optional channel attention module weight it; a lightweight segmentation head predicts a tumor mask that gates a tumor-region pooling; and a learned two-way fusion combines the global and tumor-region features before the classifier. Monte Carlo dropout over T passes supplies predictive entropy and mutual information at inference time.
[P007] This paper is not a positive result. The manuscript we originally wrote reported TAMNet at 96.45 % internal accuracy, 99.16 % on an external Kaggle set and 97.70 % on BRISC, ahead of or competitive with every baseline. An audit of the evaluation protocol showed why. The historical split shares near-duplicate slices between train and test: 1242 of 1311 Kaggle testing images (94.7 %) also occur in the training pool, and the paper's own validation scheme produces a set that is 100 % single-class. Those numbers are traceable to artifacts, but they measure leakage, not generalization. Re-measuring the same models under a manifest-pinned, near-duplicate-free protocol changes the conclusion: TAMNet no longer wins.
[P008] The contributions of this paper are:
[P009] •  A leakage audit and a clean protocol. We quantify exact and perceptual near-duplicate overlap between every pair of data sources, publish de-duplication keep-lists for both public test sets, and pin the split by SHA-256 in the configuration of every run.
[P010] •  A controlled re-evaluation. 41 runs, one to five seeds per configuration, identical training budget, the 1672-image test set evaluated once per final configuration, headline = mean over seeds with sample standard deviation (ddof = 1), and a paired bootstrap 95 % confidence interval for every headline comparison.
[P011] •  An ablation of the tumor-aware machinery. Fusion mode, channel attention and the segmentation-loss weight are each varied while the protocol is held fixed, so the contribution of each component can be read directly.
[P012] •  Corrected uncertainty, calibration and robustness measurements. A single MC-dropout protocol (BatchNorm frozen, only dropout active, T = 10), ECE with a stated bin count, and a formally defined MRRS over a fully listed corruption set.
[P013] •  Honest reporting. We state that TAMNet does not beat modern backbones here, we do not report the de-duplicated external numbers as if they were independent, and we release every intermediate artifact needed to reproduce the negative result.
[P014] II.  RELATED WORK
[P015] A.  Deep Learning for Brain Tumor MRI Analysis
[P016] Convolutional networks for brain tumor analysis were established by Havaei et al. [4] and the BRATS benchmark [2], and the U-Net family [7], nnU-Net [6] and 3-D multi-scale designs [5] now define the segmentation baseline. On the classification side, hand-crafted and shallow features were progressively replaced by end-to-end CNNs [8], and reviews of the field appear in [1] and [3]. More recent work pushes segmentation into the frequency domain [24], adds shape and scale awareness [21], and handles missing modalities [22] and modality completeness [20]. Transformer-based architectures [14], [15] and modern ConvNets [13] have since become the default classifiers in computer vision and are the backbones we compare against.
[P017] B.  Attention and Multi-Task Feature Learning
[P018] Squeeze-and-excitation [16] and CBAM [17] showed that re-weighting channels and spatial locations improves recognition at little cost; TAMNet's spatial attention follows the CBAM ordering. Attention-gated segmentation [18], nested U-Net variants [19] and multi-task segmentation-guided representations are the closest relatives of the tumor-aware pooling proposed here. Multi-task learning with an auxiliary segmentation objective is widely assumed to regularize classification, but the assumption is rarely isolated by an ablation — which is precisely what Section IV-E does. Gradient-based attribution such as Grad-CAM [33] provides a complementary, class-specific view of where a classifier looks, and we use it as the reference against which the learned attention map is compared in Section IV-J.
[P019] C.  Uncertainty Quantification and Reliability
[P020] Bayesian approximations to neural network uncertainty [25], the distinction between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty [26] and deep ensembles [27] are the standard toolkit. Calibration of modern networks is poor by default [31] and degrades further under distribution shift [28], [32]; temperature scaling and reliability diagnostics are the usual remedies. We follow this literature by reporting ECE, Brier score, NLL, predictive entropy and mutual information together, rather than any single scalar.
[P021] D.  Benchmark Validity
[P022] A less common but decisive consideration is whether the benchmark itself measures what it claims. We find that the historical split for this task shares near-duplicate slices across the train/test boundary, that a substantial fraction of a widely used public “external” test set is byte-identical to training images, and that the paper's validation scheme yields a single-class set. Because our splits are image-level rather than patient-level, every interval we report is an image-level interval; we say so explicitly wherever it matters. Corruption robustness is evaluated with the common corruption suite of [37].
[P023] E.  Research Gap
[P024] The gap this paper addresses is therefore not “no one has fused global and tumor-region features.” It is that the claimed benefit of tumor-aware multi-task fusion has not been demonstrated under a protocol that removes leakage, and that its components have not been ablated. We close that gap with a negative result: under a clean, manifest-pinned protocol, the added machinery does not improve classification, and a plain DenseNet-121 with 2.07 M fewer parameters matches TAMNet exactly.
[P025] III.  PROPOSED METHOD
[P026] 
[P027] Fig. 1. Executed TAMNet architecture. Every tensor shape in the diagram was captured with a forward hook on TumorAwareMultiTaskNet in eval mode (batch 1, 224×224 input); the dashed path carries no gradient in the executed configuration (D2). (source: paper/figs/fig1_architecture.png).
[P028] This section states the executed model exactly as implemented in the source module models/tumor_aware.py. Where an earlier draft of this paper disagreed with the code, the code is the specification and the text has been corrected; the five corrections are called out explicitly.
[P029] A.  Notation and Shared Feature Encoding
[P030] Let x in R^(3xHxW) be an input MRI slice resized to 224×224. A shared encoder E (DenseNet-121 [11] by default) produces a feature map F = E(x) in R^(C×7×7) with C = 1024. Global average pooling of F gives the global feature vector g in R^C, which is normalized by a batch-normalization layer:
[P031] g  =  BN( GAP(F) )  ∈ R^C
[P032] where GAP denotes global average pooling over the spatial dimensions. Two branches consume F: the global branch above and the tumor-region branch described next. Throughout we use z_g for the projected global feature and z_t for the tumor-region feature, so that the symbol F is reserved for the convolutional feature map. (An earlier version of the manuscript used F for both, which is the notation clash corrected here.)
[P033] B.  Tumor-Aware Spatial Attention (M_s)
[P034] The spatial attention module M_s re-weights the feature map before any pooling. It computes channel-wise mean and max statistics, concatenates them, and passes them through a 7×7 convolution followed by a sigmoid:
[P035] M_s(F)  =  F  ⊙  σ( Conv7×7( [ mean_c(F) ; max_c(F) ] ) )
[P036] where ⊙ is element-wise multiplication and the sigmoid output is a single-channel spatial map in (0, 1). This is the CBAM spatial block [17] applied to the encoder output.
[P037] C.  Channel Attention (M_c)
[P038] An SE-style channel attention module [16] is available as an option:
[P039] M_c(F)  =  F  ⊙  σ( FC(avgpool(F)) + FC(maxpool(F)) )
[P040] with the shared two-layer bottleneck at reduction 16. When enabled it is applied after M_s, i.e. spatial then channel, following CBAM order.
[P041] Correction 1 (D1). In the executed model this module is disabled: use_channel_attention defaults to False and the historical runs never wired it in. Every result labelled “TAMNet” in this paper therefore uses M_s only. The variant that enables M_c is reported separately as an ablation in Section IV-E, not as the proposed model.
[P042] D.  Tumor-Region Pooling
[P043] When a tumor mask is available it is interpolated to the resolution of F and used to gate the map before attention:
[P044] F_m = F ⊙ m,     x_s = M_s(F_m),     x_sc = M_c(x_s)
[P045] t  =  MLP( GAP(x_sc) )  ∈ R^(d_f)
[P046] where m is the mask, MLP is Linear(C → d_f) followed by BatchNorm1d, ReLU and Dropout(0.2), and d_f = 256 is the fusion dimension. Note that pooling is applied after attention and that an MLP follows the pool — an earlier draft wrote only t = GAP(F_t), which under-specifies the executed computation.
[P047] Correction 3 (D6). The mask gate m is never exercised. The training loop calls the model as model(images, return_seg=True) or model(images) and never supplies seg_mask, so mask is None in every training call and every inference call. The tumor-region branch therefore pools the unmasked feature map: what makes it tumor-aware is the attention module, not a spatial mask. The segmentation head is used only to produce L_seg and the segmentation evaluation of Section IV-G.
[P048] E.  Global–Tumor Feature Fusion (Φ)
[P049] Both branches are projected to the fusion dimension and combined by a learned two-way convex combination:
[P050] z_g = W_g g,    z_t = W_t t
[P051] w    = softmax( MLP_fuse( [ z_g ; z_t ] ) )  ∈ (0,1)^2
[P052] z    = LayerNorm( w_0 · z_g  +  w_1 · z_t )
[P053] MLP_fuse is Linear(2d_f → d_f) → ReLU → Linear(d_f → 2) → Softmax. The classifier is then a three-layer MLP on z (256 → 128 → 4) with dropout 0.3 and 0.15.
[P054] Correction 2 (D4). An earlier draft described this fusion as a concatenation of g and t. It is not: it is a softmax-weighted convex combination followed by LayerNorm. Concatenation is the ablation (use_attention_fusion = False) and is reported as such. A third variant, fusion_mode = t, passes only z_t to the classifier.
[P055] Measured over 860 slices, the learned weights are α_global = 0.9707 and α_tumor = 0.0293 (± 0.0468). The fusion therefore collapses onto the global pathway: the tumor-region feature contributes about three percent of the fused representation. Section IV-J reports the consequence directly.
[P056] F.  Auxiliary Segmentation Branch
[P057] A lightweight decoder reads the 7×7 encoder map alone — no skip connections — and upsamples it to a segmentation logit map at 56×56, which the forward pass then interpolates to the input resolution:
[P058] Conv3×3(1024→128) → BN → ReLU → up×4 → Conv3×3(128→64) → BN → ReLU → up×2 → Conv1×1(64→K)
[P059] with K segmentation classes; the result is bilinearly interpolated to 224×224. A multi-scale U-Net-style decoder over tapped encoder stages exists as an ablation but is not the executed configuration.
[P060] Correction 4 (D2, D3). In the historical training runs no runner ever passed segmentation targets, so L_seg was identically zero and the branch received no gradient. The whole-tumor / tumor-core / enhancing-tumor Dice values quoted in the earlier draft (0.9005 / 0.5803 / 0.6010) do not come from this branch at all: they are produced by a standalone 3-D U-Net trained on BraTS volumes (checkpoints/segmentation/unet3d). Section IV-G reports both facts and gives the segmentation numbers this architecture can actually defend.
[P061] G.  Multi-Task Objective
[P062] Training minimizes a weighted sum of a smoothed classification loss and, when segmentation targets are supplied, a segmentation loss:
[P063] L  =  λ_cls · L_cls  +  λ_seg · L_seg
[P064] L_cls = CrossEntropy( logits, y ; label_smoothing = 0.1 )
[P065] with λ_cls = 1.0 by default and λ_seg the weight swept in Section IV-E. L_seg is the Dice-based segmentation loss of the multi-task trainer. The Monte Carlo dropout head (UncertaintyHead) is present in the module list but its output is not added to any loss: it contributes nothing to training and exists only so that dropout sites are registered on the fused feature. This is finding D5 and is the reason pre-audit uncertainty numbers could not be salvaged.
[P066] H.  Uncertainty Quantification Using Monte Carlo Dropout
[P067] At inference the model is put in evaluation mode and only the dropout modules are re-activated, so BatchNorm uses its running statistics and every pass differs solely by dropout masks [25]. With T passes we obtain probabilities p_t(y | x):
[P068] p(y | x)      =  (1/T) Σ_t p_t(y | x)
[P069] H[p]          =  – Σ_y p(y|x) log p(y|x)              (predictive entropy)
[P070] MI[y,θ|x] =  H[p] – (1/T) Σ_t H[p_t]             (mutual information)
[P071] Correction 5 (D5). An earlier implementation called train() on the whole module, which also placed BatchNorm in training mode; the ten passes then mixed batch-statistics noise into every uncertainty number and mutated the running statistics. All uncertainty figures in this paper are produced by the corrected protocol with T = 10 and BatchNorm frozen. Dropout is active at four sites: classifier 0.3 and 0.15, tumor-pool MLP 0.2, uncertainty head 0.3.
[P072] I.  Training and Inference Procedure
[P073] Training uses AdamW with cosine annealing, label smoothing 0.1, and early stopping on validation weighted-F1. Full hyperparameters and the hardware are listed in Section IV-B. For evaluation the checkpoint with the best validation weighted-F1 is loaded, the held-out test set is streamed batch-by-batch to the GPU, and metrics are computed once from the saved predictions. Predictions are retained for every run so that all confidence intervals in this paper are recomputed from the same files rather than copied from summaries.

[P095] C.  Evaluation Metrics
[P096] Classification is scored by accuracy, macro-F1 and weighted-F1, all computed with scikit-learn conventions from the saved predictions; macro-F1 is the unweighted mean of the four per-class F1 scores, so it is never forced to exceed accuracy. Segmentation is scored by Dice and IoU on tumour slices, pixel accuracy, and the rate at which empty ground-truth masks are predicted empty. Calibration uses ECE with a stated 15 bins, maximum calibration error, NLL and Brier score; uncertainty uses predictive entropy, mutual information and the AUROC of mutual information for separating errors from correct predictions. Robustness uses the mean relative robustness score defined in Section IV-I. Computational cost uses parameter count, checkpoint size in MiB and synchronised latency. Every accuracy in the paper carries a percentile bootstrap 95 % interval from B = 1000 resamples of the test images.

[P119] Three conclusions follow. (i) Fusion. Replacing the learned convex combination with plain concatenation changes accuracy by +0.42 pp and dropping the global branch entirely changes it by -0.31 pp; neither is an improvement, and both are inside the seed-to-seed spread of ±1.10 pp. The fusion mechanism is not carrying the result.
[P120] (ii) Channel attention. Enabling M_c raises accuracy by +0.60 pp over the executed configuration (0.9420 ± 0.0059 vs 0.9360 ± 0.0110), which is the largest single component effect in the table — and it is an effect the executed model never had. We report it rather than fold it into the headline.
[P121] (iii) Segmentation loss. On the BRISC protocol, where aligned masks exist, adding the auxiliary loss never helps: λ_seg = 0 gives 0.9043 and λ_seg = 1 gives 0.8780, with the intermediate values no better. Since these are single-seed runs they are point estimates, but the direction is consistent — segmentation supervision does not improve classification. This is the central design assumption of the model, and it is not supported.

[P176] V.  DISCUSSION
[P177] Does TAMNet beat its baselines? No. Under the clean protocol the model scores 0.9360 ± 0.0110 against 0.9360 ± 0.0125 for a plain DenseNet-121 trained identically — the same accuracy with 2.08 M more parameters — and it is significantly behind EfficientNetV2-S, ConvNeXt-Tiny, Swin-T and ResNet-50. The earlier draft already lost to DenseNet-121 (96.45 % against 97.95 %); the clean protocol turns that loss into a tie. The accuracy–parameter plane in Fig. 7 makes the practical consequence explicit: there is no efficiency argument to fall back on.
[P178] What the ablations say. None of the three tumor-aware components earns its complexity on classification. The learned fusion collapses onto the global pathway (α_tumor = 0.0293); the spatial attention map places less mass inside the tumour mask than a uniform map would; and the auxiliary segmentation loss does not improve accuracy at any weight we tried. The one component with a visible positive effect is channel attention, which is disabled in the executed model — an honest reading is that the historical configuration was not the one the manuscript described.
[P179] What survives. Three things do. First, the model is genuinely more robust to corruption than DenseNet-121 (MRRS 0.8508 ± 0.0035 vs 0.8142 ± 0.0260), which is a real if modest property. Second, its mutual information separates errors from correct predictions meaningfully (error AUROC around 0.72), so the uncertainty machinery is usable for ranking risk even though the confidence values themselves are under-confident. Third, the framework genuinely does all of this in one pass with a moderate parameter budget — but that is an engineering property, not a performance claim.
[P180] Why the earlier numbers were so high. They were traceable, not invented — every one of them matched an artifact. They were simply measuring a different, leaky protocol. Re-introducing that split reproduces 0.9815 immediately, which is the clearest demonstration that the gap between the two protocols, not any change in the model, explains the entire difference. This is the methodological point of the paper: the same architecture, the same code and the same data produce a 4.5-point swing purely from how the split is drawn.
[P181] VI.  LIMITATIONS
[P182] •  Public data only. All experiments use publicly released 2-D MRI slices. There is no prospective, institutional or clinical validation of any kind.
[P183] •  Image-level, not patient-level, splits. The internal pool carries no patient identifiers, so near-duplicate control is the only available defence against patient leakage; residual within-patient correlation cannot be ruled out and every interval in this paper is image-level.
[P184] •  Unresolved label provenance. The origin of the four-class labels in the internal pool is not documented anywhere in the repository. Part of that pool shares filenames with a public Kaggle release; the remainder comes from at least three other naming families. The class counts are stated, the origin is marked [CITATION NEEDED].
[P185] •  Domain gap. The segmentation evidence comes from a different modality stack (BraTS 3-D multimodal NIfTI) and a different 2-D source (BRISC), while classification uses single-channel JPEG slices. No experiment here establishes transfer between them.
[P186] •  Intensity sensitivity. Corruption robustness is measured, but the model remains sensitive to intensity-type perturbations; the corruption suite does not cover motion, bias field or scanner domain shift.
[P187] •  Uneven seed counts. The headline configurations carry four to five seeds, but ConvNeXt-Tiny, ResNet-50, ViT-B/16 and the λ_seg sweep have a single seed each; those rows are point estimates and are labelled as such throughout.
[P188] •  Statistical resolution. Per-run bootstrap intervals are about ±1.1 pp, so this benchmark cannot resolve differences smaller than roughly one percentage point.
[P189] VII.  CONCLUSION
[P190] This paper presented TAMNet, a tumor-aware multi-task framework that couples a shared DenseNet-121 encoder with spatial attention, an auxiliary segmentation branch, a learned global–tumor fusion and Monte Carlo dropout, and then re-measured it under a leakage-audited protocol. The re-measurement is the contribution: 41 runs, 1672 held-out test images, mean-over-seeds headlines with sample standard deviations, paired bootstrap intervals on every comparison, and de-duplicated external evaluation.
[P191] The answer to the research question is negative. TAMNet reaches 0.9360 ± 0.0110 accuracy and 0.9355 ± 0.0109 macro-F1, statistically indistinguishable from plain DenseNet-121 (0.9360 ± 0.0125) and significantly below four modern backbones. Removing near-duplicates from the public test sets costs 3.57 pp on Kaggle and 4.00 pp on BRISC. The auxiliary segmentation loss provides no classification gain, the attention map does not concentrate on tumour regions, and the fusion weights ignore the tumor-region feature. The whole-tumour Dice figures quoted previously belong to a separate 3-D U-Net and are re-attributed accordingly.
[P192] What remains useful is a recipe that is cheap, moderately robust to corruption, and carries a usable error-ranking uncertainty signal — plus, we hope, a worked example of how quickly a positive result can disappear once the split is audited. Future work should obtain a genuinely patient-level dataset, make the segmentation branch receive gradient during classification training, and test whether explicit attention supervision can move the fusion weight off the global pathway.
