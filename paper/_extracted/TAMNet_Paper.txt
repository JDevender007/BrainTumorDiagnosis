[P000] TAMNet: Tumor-Aware Multi-Task Representation Learning for Brain MRI Classification and Segmentation with Uncertainty Quantification
[P001] ABSTRACT— Accurate brain tumor analysis from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is important for automated diagnosis and tumor characterization. However, existing deep learning approaches commonly optimize tumor classification and segmentation as separate tasks, while providing limited information about prediction reliability. To address these limitations, we propose TAMNet (Tumor-Aware Multi-Task Representation), a unified framework for brain MRI classification and segmentation with uncertainty quantification. TAMNet employs a shared convolutional encoder with spatial and channel attention to learn tumor-aware representations and combines them with global image features for four-class classification of glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and no-tumor cases. An auxiliary segmentation branch provides region-level supervision to encourage spatially meaningful representation learning. Monte Carlo dropout is incorporated during inference to quantify predictive uncertainty from stochastic predictions. TAMNet achieves 96.45% accuracy on the internal test set and 97.70% and 99.16% accuracy on two independent external datasets, together with a whole-tumor Dice score of 0.9005. The framework contains approximately 9.03 million trainable parameters and achieves 48.63 ms inference latency under the evaluated setting. These results indicate that tumor-aware attention and multi-task representation learning can jointly exploit global and spatially localized information while providing additional measures of prediction reliability for brain MRI analysis.

[P003] I. INTRODUCTION
[P004] Brain tumors comprise a heterogeneous group of intracranial diseases with substantial variation in tissue characteristics, anatomical location, morphology, and appearance on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Accurate identification and delineation of tumors from MRI are important for diagnosis, treatment planning, and disease assessment. MRI is particularly valuable for brain tumor analysis because of its superior soft-tissue contrast and ability to capture detailed structural information. Consequently, automated brain MRI analysis has become an active research area, with deep learning methods increasingly applied to tumor classification, localization, and segmentation [1]–[9]. Despite these advances, reliable automated tumor analysis remains challenging because tumor size, shape, intensity, and anatomical context can vary substantially across subjects and imaging conditions.
[P005] Brain tumor classification and segmentation provide complementary information for automated analysis. Classification determines the diagnostic category of an MRI image, whereas segmentation provides spatial information about tumor location and extent. Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated effectiveness for brain tumor segmentation [1], [4], [5], while benchmark datasets such as BraTS have supported systematic development and evaluation of segmentation methods [2]. Encoder–decoder architectures such as U-Net established an effective paradigm for combining contextual and spatial information in medical image segmentation [7], and nnU-Net demonstrated the importance of adaptive preprocessing, network configuration, and training strategies for biomedical segmentation [6]. In parallel, CNN-based methods have achieved promising performance in brain tumor classification [8], [9]. However, the two tasks impose different representation requirements: classification emphasizes discriminative image-level characteristics, whereas segmentation requires spatially localized representations that describe tumor regions and boundaries. Learning these complementary characteristics within a common representation space remains challenging.
[P006] The quality of learned representations also depends on the ability of a network to identify informative spatial regions and feature channels. Conventional CNNs progressively learn hierarchical features, but the resulting representations may contain information that is not equally relevant to tumor characterization. Attention mechanisms provide a means of selectively emphasizing informative features. Squeeze-and-Excitation networks introduced channel-wise feature recalibration [16], while the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) jointly models channel and spatial attention [17]. Attention-based architectures have also been investigated for medical image segmentation to emphasize task-relevant anatomical regions [18]. Meanwhile, residual networks, densely connected networks, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, and vision transformers provide alternative strategies for learning discriminative image representations [10]–[15]. These developments demonstrate the importance of both strong global feature extraction and mechanisms that can emphasize task-relevant information.
[P007] Recent brain tumor studies have further investigated specialized representations for segmentation. Methods addressing incomplete multimodal imaging have examined how available modalities can be exploited while limiting detrimental information [20], whereas shape-scale modeling has been used to capture complementary tumor structures at different spatial scales [21]. Disentanglement-based methods have attempted to separate modality-related and tumor-related information [22]. More recent approaches have investigated hierarchical label relationships and frequency-domain characteristics for brain tumor segmentation [23], [24]. These studies highlight the importance of preserving spatially meaningful tumor information. Nevertheless, much of this research remains segmentation-oriented, whereas classification approaches generally emphasize image-level discriminative learning [8], [9]. Integrating global image characteristics with tumor-focused spatial representations therefore provides an opportunity for more unified brain MRI analysis.
[P008] Prediction reliability represents an additional challenge in medical imaging. High classification accuracy does not necessarily indicate that predicted probabilities are well calibrated or that incorrect predictions can be identified reliably. Monte Carlo dropout provides a practical Bayesian approximation for quantifying predictive uncertainty through stochastic forward passes [25]. Deep ensembles provide an alternative approach for uncertainty estimation [27], while Bayesian deep learning has investigated different sources of uncertainty in computer vision [26]. Dataset shift can further alter predictive reliability when models are evaluated on distributions that differ from the training data [28]. Uncertainty-aware approaches have therefore been investigated for brain tumor segmentation and multimodal medical image analysis [29], [30]. Calibration measures such as expected calibration error provide complementary information regarding the correspondence between predicted confidence and observed correctness [31], [32]. These considerations motivate evaluating brain tumor models beyond conventional accuracy-based measures.
[P009] Motivated by these challenges, we propose TAMNet, a unified framework for brain MRI classification and segmentation with uncertainty quantification. TAMNet uses a shared convolutional encoder with spatial and channel attention to derive tumor-aware representations while retaining global image information. The complementary representations are fused for four-class classification of glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and no-tumor cases. An auxiliary segmentation branch provides region-level supervision during training to encourage spatially meaningful representations. During inference, Monte Carlo dropout produces stochastic predictions that are used to quantify predictive uncertainty. The framework is evaluated using internal and independent external datasets, together with segmentation analysis, calibration assessment, robustness testing, interpretability analysis, multi-seed evaluation, and computational efficiency measurements. TAMNet achieves 96.45% internal test accuracy, 97.70% and 99.16% accuracy on two independent external datasets, and a whole-tumor Dice score of 0.9005. These results support a unified approach for tumor-aware representation learning that jointly considers classification, segmentation, and predictive reliability in brain MRI analysis.
[P010] II. RELATED WORK
[P011] A. Deep Learning for Brain Tumor MRI Analysis
[P012] Deep learning has become an important approach for automated brain tumor analysis because of its ability to learn hierarchical representations directly from MRI data. Early studies demonstrated the effectiveness of CNNs for brain tumor segmentation by learning discriminative spatial features from MR images [1], [4], [5]. The Multimodal Brain Tumor Image Segmentation Benchmark (BraTS) established an important basis for systematic development and evaluation of automated tumor segmentation methods [2]. U-Net introduced an encoder–decoder architecture that combines contextual and spatial information for biomedical segmentation [7], while nnU-Net demonstrated the influence of adaptive preprocessing, network configuration, and training strategies on biomedical segmentation performance [6].
[P013] Deep learning has also been widely investigated for brain tumor classification. CNN-based approaches have demonstrated the ability to learn discriminative features for multi-class tumor categorization [8], while recent studies have reviewed and compared machine-learning and deep-learning approaches for MRI-based tumor classification [9]. These studies demonstrate the effectiveness of learned representations for both classification and segmentation. However, the two tasks require different forms of information. Classification primarily relies on image-level discriminative characteristics, whereas segmentation requires spatially localized representations describing tumor regions and boundaries. Consequently, independently optimizing the two tasks may limit the exploitation of their complementary information.
[P014] B. Attention-Based Feature Representation
[P015] The effectiveness of medical image analysis depends on identifying informative spatial regions and feature channels. Conventional CNNs learn hierarchical representations, but not all extracted features contribute equally to tumor characterization. Attention mechanisms address this limitation by selectively emphasizing informative feature components. Squeeze-and-Excitation networks introduced channel-wise feature recalibration to model inter-channel dependencies [16]. CBAM further combines channel and spatial attention to emphasize informative feature channels and image regions [17]. Attention U-Net demonstrated the use of attention mechanisms for focusing segmentation networks on task-relevant anatomical structures [18].
[P016] Several modern architectures have also been developed to improve image representation. Residual networks facilitate deep feature learning through residual connections [10], while DenseNet promotes feature reuse through dense connectivity [11]. EfficientNet introduced compound scaling of network dimensions [12], and ConvNeXt revisited convolutional design using principles associated with modern vision architectures [13]. Vision Transformer and Swin Transformer subsequently demonstrated attention-based modeling of long-range visual relationships [14], [15]. Although these architectures provide powerful general-purpose representations, they do not explicitly impose tumor-focused representation learning. This motivates attention mechanisms that complement global image features with spatial and channel representations emphasizing tumor-relevant information.
[P017] C. Multi-Task and Segmentation-Guided Representation Learning
[P018] Classification and segmentation provide complementary information for brain tumor analysis, motivating multi-task learning. Segmentation supervision provides explicit spatial information that can encourage a network to learn representations associated with tumor regions rather than relying exclusively on global image appearance. UNet++ demonstrated the benefits of nested feature aggregation and multiscale representation learning for medical image segmentation [19].
[P019] Recent brain tumor studies have further investigated specialized representations for tumor heterogeneity and spatial complexity. Qiu et al. examined the effects of available modalities in incomplete multimodal brain tumor segmentation [20], whereas Zhou et al. introduced shape-scale co-awareness to model complementary tumor structures at different spatial scales [21]. D2-Net investigated disentangled representations for separating modality-related and tumor-related information under missing modalities [22].
[P020] More recent TMI studies have investigated hierarchical and frequency-domain representations. Deep hierarchy-aware segmentation models relationships among tumor labels [23], while frequency-domain approaches investigate frequency characteristics for brain tumor representation [24]. These methods emphasize the importance of preserving spatially meaningful tumor information. Nevertheless, most remain primarily segmentation-oriented, whereas classification methods generally optimize image-level discriminative features. A unified representation-learning strategy that uses spatial tumor information to support classification while simultaneously learning segmentation therefore remains an important direction.
[P021] D. Uncertainty Quantification and Reliability
[P022] Prediction accuracy alone does not fully characterize the reliability of a medical imaging model. A network may produce highly confident predictions for ambiguous samples or inputs that differ from the training distribution. Monte Carlo dropout provides a practical Bayesian approximation for quantifying predictive uncertainty through repeated stochastic forward passes [25]. Deep ensembles provide another strategy by aggregating predictions from independently trained models [27], while Bayesian deep learning has investigated different sources of uncertainty in computer vision [26].
[P023] Reliability becomes particularly important under dataset shift, where model uncertainty may change when the evaluation distribution differs from the training distribution [28]. Uncertainty-aware methods have therefore been investigated for brain tumor segmentation, including approaches that use uncertainty maps together with attention-aware feature fusion [29] and mutual learning strategies for brain and brain tumor segmentation [30]. Calibration analysis provides another perspective by evaluating the correspondence between predicted confidence and observed correctness. Expected calibration error and related measures have been widely used to assess calibration in modern neural networks [31], [32]. These studies motivate the joint evaluation of predictive performance and reliability rather than relying exclusively on accuracy.
[P024] E. Research Gap
[P025] The reviewed literature demonstrates substantial progress in brain tumor classification, segmentation, attention-based representation learning, and uncertainty quantification. However, these capabilities are commonly addressed as separate components. Segmentation methods emphasize spatial delineation, classification methods focus on image-level discrimination, attention mechanisms enhance selected features, and uncertainty methods characterize prediction reliability. Their integration within a unified representation-learning framework remains comparatively limited.
[P026] TAMNet addresses this gap by combining global image representations with spatial and channel attention to obtain tumor-aware features, while an auxiliary segmentation task provides region-level supervision during representation learning. Monte Carlo dropout is further incorporated during inference to quantify predictive uncertainty. The resulting framework jointly considers discriminative classification, spatial tumor representation, segmentation supervision, and predictive reliability within a unified brain MRI analysis framework.
[P027] III. PROPOSED METHOD
[P028] TAMNet is designed to jointly learn discriminative and spatially meaningful representations from brain MRI while providing predictive uncertainty during inference. The proposed framework addresses three related requirements: image-level tumor classification, tumor-region representation learning, and prediction reliability. As illustrated conceptually in Fig. 1, an input MRI image is first processed by a shared convolutional encoder to obtain hierarchical feature representations. A tumor-aware attention mechanism then emphasizes informative spatial locations and feature channels, producing a representation that is complementary to the global image representation. These representations are fused and supplied to a four-class classification head. In parallel, an auxiliary segmentation branch predicts tumor regions and provides region-level supervision during training. Monte Carlo (MC) dropout is applied during inference to obtain stochastic predictions and quantify predictive uncertainty.

[P109] I. Design Rationale
[P110] The proposed architecture is designed around the interaction of four components rather than the independent addition of standard modules. First, the shared encoder provides hierarchical image representations. Second, spatial and channel attention transforms these representations into tumor-aware features by emphasizing informative locations and feature responses [16], [17]. Third, the auxiliary segmentation task supplies region-level supervision that encourages the shared representation to retain tumor-related spatial information. Finally, MC dropout extends the deterministic classifier with predictive uncertainty information [25].
[P111] The resulting design addresses complementary requirements of automated brain MRI analysis. Global representations preserve contextual information, tumor-aware representations emphasize localized diagnostic information, segmentation supervision introduces spatial constraints, and stochastic inference provides information about prediction reliability. TAMNet therefore provides a unified representation-learning framework in which classification and segmentation contribute jointly during training while uncertainty is quantified during inference.
[P112] The proposed framework contains approximately 9.03 million trainable parameters. Under the evaluated computational setting, the measured inference latency is 48.63 ms per image. These characteristics allow the computational cost of the proposed representation-learning strategy to be evaluated together with its predictive and uncertainty-related performance.

[P174] VI. DISCUSSION
[P175] The experimental results demonstrate that TAMNet can jointly address brain tumor classification, tumor-region representation learning, and predictive uncertainty within a unified framework. The proposed model achieved 96.45% accuracy and an F1 score of 0.9847 on the internal test set while maintaining approximately 9.03 million trainable parameters. These results indicate that the combination of global image information and tumor-aware representations can provide effective discrimination among glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and no-tumor categories. Importantly, TAMNet does not achieve the highest classification accuracy among all evaluated backbones. EfficientNetV2 and several other architectures obtain higher accuracy under the same comparison protocol [10]–[15]. The contribution of TAMNet therefore lies in integrating complementary capabilities rather than optimizing classification accuracy alone.
[P176] The backbone comparison illustrates the trade-off between predictive performance and model complexity. DenseNet121, ResNet50, EfficientNetV2, ConvNeXt-Tiny, Swin-T, and ViT-B/16 offer competitive classification performance, but TAMNet incorporates additional spatial attention, auxiliary segmentation supervision, and uncertainty quantification. Dense connectivity provides a strong baseline representation [11], while modern architectures exploit alternative scaling or attention strategies [12]–[15]. TAMNet instead emphasizes the interaction between global and tumor-aware information. This design supports a representation that is not restricted to image-level classification but also preserves spatial structure relevant to tumor analysis.
[P177] The external evaluation provides additional evidence regarding the transferability of the learned representation. TAMNet obtained 97.70% accuracy on BRISC-2025 and 99.16% accuracy on the external Kaggle dataset. On the Kaggle dataset, the F1 score, Matthews correlation coefficient, and Cohen's kappa reached 0.9916, 0.9888, and 0.9887, respectively. These results indicate consistent performance across the evaluated external datasets. However, the datasets represent only a limited range of acquisition conditions and population characteristics. Consequently, external dataset performance should be interpreted as evidence of dataset-level transfer rather than comprehensive clinical generalization.
[P178] The auxiliary segmentation branch contributes an additional spatial perspective to the representation-learning process. The whole-tumor Dice score of 0.9005 indicates effective identification of the overall tumor region, whereas lower Dice scores for tumor core and enhancing tumor, 0.5803 and 0.6010, respectively, demonstrate greater difficulty in identifying finer tumor subregions. This difference is relevant because smaller or more heterogeneous structures can be more difficult to delineate. The segmentation branch is therefore best interpreted as spatial supervision that complements classification rather than as a replacement for a dedicated multimodal segmentation architecture. This interpretation is consistent with the broader literature on U-Net-style and multi-task medical image segmentation [7], [19]–[24].
[P179] Uncertainty analysis further extends the interpretation of the classification results. Using ten stochastic MC-dropout forward passes, TAMNet produced a mean confidence of 0.8821, with higher confidence for correct predictions (0.8843) than incorrect predictions (0.8232). The mean predictive entropy was 0.4778 and mutual information was 0.0415. These observations indicate that stochastic prediction variation provides information related to prediction reliability. This is consistent with the purpose of MC dropout as a practical approximation for Bayesian uncertainty [25], while Bayesian deep learning and ensemble approaches provide broader methodological context [26], [27]. Nevertheless, the ECE of 0.0855, together with a Brier score of 0.0736 and NLL of 0.2172, indicates that predictive confidence remains imperfectly calibrated. Calibration is particularly important in medical imaging because a numerically high confidence does not inherently imply a reliable prediction [31], [32].
[P180] The robustness experiments identify another important characteristic of the framework. TAMNet remained relatively stable under small geometric perturbation, achieving 95.96% accuracy after a 5° rotation. In contrast, intensity-based perturbations caused substantially greater degradation. Contrast reduction to 0.5 reduced accuracy to 22.40%, while brightness reduction and Gaussian noise produced accuracies of 54.17% and 56.81%, respectively. These findings suggest that the learned representation retains useful geometric information but remains dependent on MRI intensity characteristics. Such sensitivity is important in the context of dataset shift and changing acquisition conditions [28]. The findings motivate future investigation of intensity normalization, domain adaptation, and targeted data augmentation.
[P181] The efficiency results provide an additional perspective on the proposed design. TAMNet contains approximately 9.03 million trainable parameters and achieves a measured latency of 48.63 ms per image. This computational profile is smaller than that of several high-capacity architectures included in the backbone study while still supporting classification, segmentation supervision, and uncertainty analysis. The result suggests that the framework offers a moderate computational cost relative to its functional scope. However, latency remains implementation-dependent, and the measured throughput should not be generalized to different hardware configurations without direct benchmarking.
[P182] Overall, the results support TAMNet as a unified tumor-aware representation-learning framework that integrates global contextual information, localized attention, segmentation supervision, predictive uncertainty, and robustness analysis. The evidence supports the usefulness of this integrated design while also identifying calibration, intensity robustness, and broader external validation as important areas for further improvement.
[P183] VII. LIMITATIONS
[P184] Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the presented results.
[P185] First, the classification and segmentation evaluations are based on different dataset sources. The internal and external datasets are used primarily for four-class classification, whereas BraTS is used for tumor segmentation. Consequently, the reported classification and segmentation results do not represent a fully paired dataset in which the same subjects simultaneously provide classification labels and detailed segmentation masks.
[P186] Second, although TAMNet was evaluated on two independent external classification datasets, the available external datasets do not encompass the full diversity of clinical imaging protocols, institutions, scanner manufacturers, demographic distributions, and disease presentations. Broader multi-center validation would therefore be required to characterize generalization more comprehensively.
[P187] Third, the robustness experiments reveal substantial sensitivity to intensity-related perturbations. In particular, contrast reduction produces a much larger performance degradation than small geometric perturbations. This indicates that additional preprocessing, normalization, or domain-adaptation strategies may be necessary for heterogeneous MRI acquisition conditions.
[P188] Fourth, uncertainty estimation is based on 10 MC-dropout stochastic passes. A larger number of stochastic samples or alternative approaches, such as deep ensembles, could provide additional information about uncertainty behavior [27]. Furthermore, the ECE result indicates that the predicted probabilities are not perfectly calibrated, suggesting that post-hoc or training-time calibration methods could be investigated [31], [32].
[P189] Fifth, the multi-seed evaluation uses only three independent seeds. Although the observed variability is relatively small, a larger number of repeated experiments together with subject-level statistical analysis would provide stronger evidence regarding reproducibility.
[P190] Finally, the measured inference latency depends on the evaluated computational environment, implementation, and input configuration. Therefore, the reported 48.63 ms latency and 20.6 FPS throughput should be interpreted as measurements under the evaluated setting rather than hardware-independent deployment characteristics.
[P191] VIII. CONCLUSION
[P192] This paper presented TAMNet, a tumor-aware multi-task representation learning framework for brain MRI classification and segmentation with uncertainty quantification. The proposed approach combines a shared DenseNet121-based encoder with spatial and channel attention to learn tumor-focused representations while retaining global image information. An auxiliary segmentation branch provides region-level supervision, and Monte Carlo dropout enables predictive uncertainty analysis during inference.
[P193] TAMNet achieved 96.45% classification accuracy and an F1 score of 0.9847 on the internal test set, with 97.70% and 99.16% accuracy on the evaluated BRISC-2025 and Kaggle external datasets, respectively. The model obtained a whole-tumor Dice score of 0.9005, while tumor-core and enhancing-tumor Dice scores were 0.5803 and 0.6010, respectively. Uncertainty analysis indicated lower mean confidence for incorrect predictions than for correct predictions, although calibration remained imperfect. Robustness experiments revealed relatively limited degradation under small geometric perturbation but substantial sensitivity to intensity-related distortions.
[P194] With approximately 9.03 million trainable parameters and 48.63 ms measured inference latency, TAMNet provides a moderate computational profile while integrating multiple analysis capabilities within a single framework. Future work should investigate stronger calibration, improved robustness to intensity and domain variations, larger multi-center datasets, and more comprehensive uncertainty evaluation to support reliable application of unified brain MRI analysis frameworks.
